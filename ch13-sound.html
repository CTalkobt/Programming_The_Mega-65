Programming the MEGA65 — Chapter 13: Sound

Oct 4, 2026 · @Craig Taylor

Introduction

The C64 had one SID. The MEGA65 has four, giving twelve voices, plus four channels of digitised sound played by DMA. Everything passes through a programmable cross-bar mixer before reaching the 3.5mm socket and the digital video output. The mixer also has an input reserved for an OPL-compatible FM synthesiser, but the MEGA65 Book notes that this device is not currently functional in the MEGA65 core (MEGA65 Book, p. V-24), so it is not covered here.

The two working kinds of sound are very different. A SID synthesises its waveforms from a handful of register settings, so music costs almost no memory or CPU time. Digitised sound reproduces recorded audio, such as speech, real instruments or sound effects, at the cost of memory: each second of sound is thousands of bytes.

This chapter covers:

  • Sound and music from BASIC 65, which needs no register knowledge
  • The four SIDs, their placement and the 6581/8580 choice
  • The SID registers
  • Digital audio through the audio DMA channels
  • The cross-bar mixer and its registers
  • A complete example of sample playback

Register details in this chapter follow the MEGA65 Book, the official reference, in its June 2025 edition. Page references such as "p. P-18" refer to that edition.

Sound from BASIC 65

BASIC 65 divides the SIDs between two jobs. PLAY uses SID 1 for voices 1-3 and SID 3 for voices 4-6; SOUND uses SID 2 for voices 1-3 and SID 4 for voices 4-6. Music and sound effects therefore never interrupt each other (MEGA65 Book, pp. B-190, B-243). Both commands return immediately and play in the background under interrupt control, unlike the C128, which stopped the program while it played.

Command Format Purpose
SOUND SOUND voice, freq, dur [, dir, min, sweep, wave, pulse] Sound effect on SID 2 or 4
SOUND CLR SOUND CLR Silence SOUND and PLAY and reset the sound system
PLAY PLAY [string1, ..., string6] Music on SID 1 and 3
TEMPO TEMPO speed Music speed, 1-255
ENVELOPE ENVELOPE n [, attack, decay, sustain, release, waveform, pw] Redefine instrument slot 0-9
FILTER FILTER sid [, freq, lp, bp, hp, res] Set the filter for the right or left music SID
VOL VOL right, left Volume 0-15 for SIDs 1-2 and SIDs 3-4
RPLAY RPLAY(voice) 1 if music voice 1-6 is playing, otherwise 0

SOUND

The voice is 1 to 6. The frequency is the raw 16-bit SID frequency value, 0 to 65535, not a value in hertz; the next section shows how to convert. The duration is in jiffies, 0 to 32767: 50 per second in PAL video mode and 60 in NTSC.

The optional parameters add a sweep and choose the tone. The direction is 0 up, 1 down or 2 oscillating; min is the lowest frequency of the sweep and sweep its range. The waveform is 0 triangle, 1 sawtooth, 2 square (the default) or 3 noise, and pulse sets the square wave's width, 0 to 4095 (MEGA65 Book, p. B-243).

Because jiffies depend on the video standard, a program that wants a sound of fixed length should check it first. Bit 7 of $D06F is set in NTSC mode:

10 IF PEEK($D06F) AND $80 THEN J=60 : ELSE J=50
20 SOUND 1, 7382, J                      : REM SQUARE WAVE FOR ONE SECOND
30 SOUND 3, 4000, 120, 2, 2000, 400, 1   : REM SWEEPING SAWTOOTH
40 SOUND CLR                             : REM SILENCE AND RESET

These lines follow the Book's own example (MEGA65 Book, p. B-243).

PLAY

PLAY takes up to six strings, one per music voice. A note is a letter A to G, optionally preceded by modifiers. Directives are a letter followed by a digit, and stay in force until changed (MEGA65 Book, pp. B-190, B-191).

Modifier Effect
# Sharp
$ Flat
. Dotted; it goes before the note, unlike sheet music
W H Q I S Whole, half, quarter, eighth or sixteenth note
R Rest
Directive Meaning Range
O Octave; 4 is the default, middle octave 0-6
T Instrument envelope 0-9
U Volume 0-9
X Filter off or on 0-1
M Modulation: vibrato depth, 0 off 0-9
P Portamento: slide speed between notes, 0 off 0-9
L Loop back to the start of the string none

While portamento is on, notes are not gated off between each other; turn it off with P0 to restore normal note endings.

10 ENVELOPE 9, 10, 5, 10, 5, 0, 300
20 VOL 8, 8
30 TEMPO 30
40 PLAY "O5T9HCIDCDEHCG IGAGFEFDEWCL", "O2T0QCGEGCGEG DBGB CGEGL"

This example is from the Book (MEGA65 Book, p. B-191). Spaces in PLAY strings are ignored, so they can separate bars.

Empty arguments leave a voice playing undisturbed, so PLAY ,"O5CDE" changes only voice 2. PLAY with no arguments silences all voices and resets the music system, including TEMPO. A PLAY string is an ordinary BASIC string and limited to 255 characters; longer pieces are played in sections, using RPLAY to wait for each to finish.

TEMPO, ENVELOPE, FILTER and VOL

TEMPO sets the length of a whole note to 24 divided by the speed, in seconds (MEGA65 Book, p. B-262): TEMPO 24 makes a whole note last one second.

ENVELOPE redefines one of the ten instrument slots used by PLAY's T directive. Attack, decay, sustain and release are 0 to 15; the waveform is 0 triangle, 1 sawtooth, 2 pulse, 3 noise or 4 ring modulation; pw is the pulse width (MEGA65 Book, p. B-99). The defaults are:

Slot Instrument A D S R Wave Pulse
0 Piano 0 9 0 0 2 1536
1 Accordion 12 0 12 0 1
2 Calliope 0 0 15 0 0
3 Drum 0 5 5 0 3
4 Flute 9 4 4 0 0
5 Guitar 0 9 2 1 1
6 Harpsichord 0 9 0 0 2 512
7 Organ 0 9 9 0 2 2048
8 Trumpet 8 9 4 1 2 512
9 Xylophone 0 9 0 0 0

FILTER sets the SID filter for music: sid is 1 for the right music SID and 2 for the left. The cutoff is 0 to 2047, the three mode switches are 0 or 1, and resonance is 0 to 15. Combining modes produces band-reject and notch effects. A voice uses the filter only while its PLAY string has X1 in effect (MEGA65 Book, p. B-109).

VOL sets the volume, 0 to 15, of the right pair of SIDs, 1 and 2, and the left pair, 3 and 4. "Right" and "left" describe the default mix; the user's mixer settings decide where each pair is actually heard (MEGA65 Book, p. B-279).

The Four SIDs

The MEGA65's SIDs are implemented in the FPGA, and each behaves as a complete SID with three voices, a filter and its own envelope generators. They occupy four consecutive 32-byte register blocks, and the Book's I/O map names them by the side of the mix they feed:

SID Registers Book name Used by BASIC for
1 $D400-$D41F Right #1 PLAY voices 1-3
2 $D420-$D43F Right #2 SOUND voices 1-3
3 $D440-$D45F Left #1 PLAY voices 4-6
4 $D460-$D47F Left #2 SOUND voices 4-6

The same four SIDs appear again at $D480-$D4FF (MEGA65 Book, p. J-9). Each block's registers are laid out exactly as on the C64, so the reference in the next section applies to all four: add $20, $40 or $60 to reach SIDs 2, 3 and 4.

Left and right

The mixer treats the SIDs as two pairs: one mixer input carries the left SIDs and another the right SIDs (MEGA65 Book, p. V-24). By default each pair is panned only slightly towards its side, so a C64 tune played on SID 1 is still heard from both speakers. A program that wants a part to come from one side chooses a SID from the appropriate pair; the user's mixer settings decide how far apart the two pairs actually sound.

Chip models

Commodore built two versions of the SID. The original 6581 and the later 8580 differ mainly in their filters and in how combined waveforms sound, and some C64 games relied on quirks of the 6581 that the 8580 fixed (MEGA65 Book, p. 4-7). Music written for one can sound noticeably different on the other, because composers tuned their filter settings to the chip they had.

The MEGA65 models either. The user chooses a default with the SID generation setting in the configuration utility, and a program can choose for itself with SIDMODE at $D63C: 0 selects 6581 behaviour and 1 selects 8580 (MEGA65 Book, p. R-3). The Book's register table places SIDMODE in the low four bits of $D63C, suggesting one bit per SID; confirm the bit order on hardware before relying on it. A program that changes SIDMODE should restore it before exiting, since it overrides the user's setting.

Timing music

C64 music players are usually called once per frame from a raster interrupt, which ties their tempo to the video standard: 50 frames a second in PAL, 60 in NTSC. The MEGA65 keeps both video modes and their timings, so a tune written for PAL plays 20% faster in NTSC mode. A player that must sound the same in both should be driven by a CIA timer, or check bit 7 of $D06F and adjust its tempo, as the SOUND example above does for durations.

SID Register Reference

Offsets are from the base of a SID, $D400 for SID 1. Voices 1, 2 and 3 use the same seven-register layout at offsets $00, $07 and $0E.

Playing a note

The frequency value is the output frequency multiplied by 16,777,216 and divided by the SID's clock rate, roughly 1 MHz. On a PAL C64 the clock is 985,248 Hz, so concert A at 440 Hz needs a value of about 7,493. The SID's own clock is what matters here, not the CPU speed, so the 45GS02 running at 40.5 MHz does not change the pitch.

This plays a note on SID 3, voice 1, which comes from the left of the default mix:

sid3    = $d440
        lda #$0f
        sta sid3+$18    ; volume 15, no filter
        lda #$09
        sta sid3+$05    ; attack 0, decay 9
        lda #$00
        sta sid3+$06    ; sustain 0, release 0
        lda #<7493
        sta sid3+$00
        lda #>7493
        sta sid3+$01    ; about 440 Hz
        lda #$11
        sta sid3+$04    ; triangle, gate on
        ; ... later ...
        lda #$10
        sta sid3+$04    ; triangle, gate off: release

The SID registers, apart from the four read-only ones, cannot be read back. A program that needs to know a register's current value, for example to change one control bit, should keep its own copy in RAM.

Digital Audio with Audio DMA

To play a recorded sound, a computer must send its samples to a digital-to-analogue converter at an exact, steady rate: 8,000 times a second for an 8 kHz sample. The MEGA65's DMA controller has four audio channels that do this in the background, much like the Amiga's. Unlike the Amiga's, they support 8- and 16-bit samples, rates up to about 1 MHz and 256 volume levels per channel, but no modulation between channels (MEGA65 Book, p. P-3).

How it works

The DMA controller is part of the CPU, and the MEGA65 has no bus slots reserved for audio. Audio DMA instead uses idle CPU cycles, which exist only at full speed, about 40.5 MHz: then every instruction that reads a byte from memory leaves a wait state that audio DMA can use. Two consequences follow (MEGA65 Book, p. P-18):

  • The CPU must run at full speed while audio DMA plays.
  • Tight loops need a memory read. A loop that never reads memory, such as loop: JMP loop, starves audio DMA. Add a read, such as LDA $1234, to any idle loop.

Audio DMA also keeps running during ordinary DMA jobs, stealing cycles from them so that playback is not interrupted (MEGA65 Book, p. P-3).

Global control: $D711

Bit Name Effect
7 AUDEN Enable audio DMA, and feed it to the mixer in place of the direct digital registers at $D6F8-$D6FB
4 NOMIX Bypass the mixer: audio DMA plays at full volume and all other sound, including the SIDs, is silenced

The other bits should normally be left clear, so write $80 to $D711 to enable audio DMA (MEGA65 Book, p. P-18).

Channel registers

Channels 0 to 3 have 16 registers each, at $D720, $D730, $D740 and $D750. Offsets are the same for every channel (MEGA65 Book, pp. P-21 to P-23):

Offset Name Contents
$0 control Bit 7 enable, bit 6 loop, bit 5 sign, bit 4 sine mode, bit 3 stop flag, bits 1-0 sample format
$1-$3 base address 24-bit address where a looping sample restarts
$4-$6 frequency 24-bit rate value, added to a counter every CPU cycle
$7-$8 top address Low 16 bits of the last sample's address
$9 volume 0 (mute) to $FF (full), to this channel's own side
$A-$C current address 24-bit address of the next sample; set it to start playback
$D-$F timing counter The rate counter itself

The control bits in detail:

Bit Name Meaning
7 CHxEN 1 plays the channel; clearing it stops playback
6 CHxLOOP At the end, continue from the base address
5 CHxSGN Sample sign: 0 signed, 1 unsigned
4 CHxSINE Play a built-in 32-sample 16-bit sine wave instead of memory
3 CHxSTP Set by the hardware when a sample finishes
1-0 CHxSBITS %11 16-bit, %10 8-bit, %01 upper nybble, %00 lower nybble

Three details trip people up. First, playback starts from the current address at $A-$C, not the base address; the base address is only the loop point, and for a whole-sample loop both are set to the start. Second, only the low 16 bits of the end are compared, so one sample can be at most 64KB and must lie in chip RAM. Third, the current address registers are not latched and may change while being read; stop the channel before reading them if an exact value matters (MEGA65 Book, pp. P-18, P-19).

Getting the sign bit wrong makes the waveform jump wherever it crosses zero, which sounds like loud crackling. 4-bit samples use one half of each byte, so two different 4-bit sounds can share the same memory, one in each nybble.

Sample rate

The frequency value is added to a 24-bit counter every CPU cycle, and a new sample is fetched each time the counter overflows (MEGA65 Book, p. P-19). The value for a given rate is therefore:

value = rate * 16777216 / 40500000
Sample rate Value
4 kHz $000679
8 kHz $000CF2
11.025 kHz $0011D7
16 kHz $0019E4
22.05 kHz $0023AE
32 kHz $0033C8

These values assume the Book's 40.5 MHz clock; some published tables use 40 MHz and differ by about 1%, which is inaudible for most purposes. At rates up to about 16 kHz with 8-bit samples, the irregular spacing of idle cycles causes no audible distortion in normal programs; at much higher rates it can (MEGA65 Book, p. P-19). Frequency and volume can both be changed while a sample plays, so one recorded note can be played at different pitches or faded in and out.

Stereo

Channels 0 and 1 feed the left digital audio input of the mixer and channels 2 and 3 the right, at the volume in their own register $9. Four more registers let each channel also feed the opposite side: $D71C and $D71D send channels 0 and 1 to the right, and $D71E and $D71F send channels 2 and 3 to the left. Setting both $D729 and $D71C to $FF plays channel 0 at full volume on both sides (MEGA65 Book, p. P-19).

Sine mode

Setting bit 4 of a channel's control register plays a built-in 32-sample, 16-bit sine wave instead of fetching from memory. Because no memory is read, the result is free of the distortion idle-cycle timing can cause, and clean tones well above 60 kHz are possible. The address registers must still be set as if the table sat in the bottom 64 bytes of memory (MEGA65 Book, pp. P-19, P-20).

End of sample and interrupts

When a non-looping sample ends, the hardware sets the channel's stop flag, bit 3. The Book's text states that audio DMA cannot presently generate interrupts, although its register table lists interrupt flag and enable bits at $D713 and offset registers at $D714-$D717 (MEGA65 Book, pp. P-20, P-21). Until those are documented, a program should poll the stop flag, for example once a frame.

Longer sounds

A 64KB region holds about eight seconds of 8-bit sound at 8 kHz. Longer sounds use the loop bit as a ring buffer: the channel loops over a region while the program copies the next part of the sound into the half the current address has just left, using DMA from attic RAM. Because audio DMA steals cycles from ordinary DMA jobs, the copies do not interrupt playback.

The Audio Mixer

Everything the MEGA65 plays passes through a full cross-bar mixer. Each output, such as the left speaker or the right headphone, is built by multiplying every input by its own 16-bit coefficient and adding the results. Because each output has its own complete set of coefficients, every output can carry a completely different mix (MEGA65 Book, p. V-23).

Inputs and outputs

The inputs relevant to the MEGA65 are listed below; the others exist only on the MEGAphone or development boards (MEGA65 Book, p. V-24).

Input Source
$0 Left SIDs (3 and 4)
$1 Right SIDs (1 and 2)
$8 Digital audio left: audio DMA channels 0-1, or $D6F8-$D6F9
$9 Digital audio right: audio DMA channels 2-3, or $D6FA-$D6FB
$E Coefficient also used for the OPL FM input, $F (not yet functional)
$F Its coefficient is the master volume for the output
Output Destination
$0 Left primary output
$1 Right primary output
$6 Left headphone output
$7 Right headphone output

Mixer registers

The 128 coefficients are reached through a register-select port and a data port (MEGA65 Book, p. V-26):

Register Name Purpose
$D6F4 MIXREGSEL Select a coefficient byte
$D6F5 MIXREGDATA Read or write the selected byte
$D6F8-$D6F9 DIGILLSB/MSB 16-bit sample for the left digital input
$D6FA-$D6FB DIGIRLSB/MSB 16-bit sample for the right digital input
$D6FC-$D6FD READBACKLSB/MSB Audio read-back, source selected by $D6F4, for visualisers or effects

The coefficient for input i on output o starts at byte o × 32 + i × 2: the low byte there, the high byte at the next number. For example, the right SIDs (input 1) on the left primary output (output 0) are bytes 2 and 3. Changes take effect at once, so ramp coefficients gradually to avoid clicks, and allow 32 cycles after changing one coefficient before selecting another, or the change may be lost (MEGA65 Book, pp. V-23, V-25).

This sets the right SIDs to full level on the left primary output:

        lda #2          ; output 0, input 1, low byte
        ldx #$ff
        jsr setmix
        lda #3          ; high byte
        ldx #$ff
        jsr setmix
        rts

; setmix: write X to mixer byte A, then wait
; at least 32 cycles before returning
setmix  sta $d6f4
        stx $d6f5
        ldy #8
wait    lda $d6f5       ; the read also gives audio
        dey             ; DMA its idle cycles
        bne wait
        rts

Direct digital audio

With AUDEN in $D711 clear, the digital inputs take their samples straight from $D6F8-$D6FB, 16 bits per side. A program writing these registers at a steady rate can play procedurally generated sound, such as a software synthesiser, without audio DMA. For recorded samples audio DMA is simpler, steadier and cheaper in CPU time (MEGA65 Book, p. V-25).

Working with the mix

The mixer settings belong to the user, who adjusts them with the audio mixer in the Freezer, and they persist with the machine. A program should normally control loudness and position through what it owns: each SID's volume register, the choice of SID pair, and the audio DMA volumes and cross-feed registers. Programs that genuinely need to manage the mix, such as music trackers, should read the coefficients they change and restore them before exiting.

Two practical habits help while developing:

  • Test with stereo speakers. A sound that seems centred on one speaker may come entirely from one side.
  • Keep the volume down while experimenting. A wrong sample format or bad register value can produce a very loud noise. Use speakers with their own volume control, or lower the master volume in the Freezer, rather than headphones plugged straight into the socket.

On the R3 and earlier boards, the 3.5mm socket is driven by an over-sampled 1-bit output, with a choice of pulse-width or pulse-density modulation through the PWMPDM bit in $D711. R4 and later boards use a dedicated DAC, and on older boards the Book recommends HDMI audio for the best quality (MEGA65 Book, pp. V-25, V-26).

A Worked Example: Playing a Sample

This example plays a recorded sound effect once, centred between the speakers, while the program carries on, and then detects when it has finished.

Preparing the sound

Audio DMA plays raw sample data with no file header, so a sound recorded or downloaded on a PC must first be converted. The free ffmpeg tool does it in one step; this converts any audio file to mono, 8 kHz, unsigned 8-bit samples (Dan Sanderson):

ffmpeg -i boom.wav -f u8 -ar 8000 -ac 1 boom.raw

The options are the sample format (u8 unsigned 8-bit, s16le signed 16-bit), the sample rate in hertz, and the number of channels. Copy the result to a disk image or the SD card with the program.

Loading it

From BASIC, reserve memory in bank 5 with MEM, as Chapter 6 described, then BLOAD the file to $50000:

10 MEM 0,3                       : REM RESERVE $50000-$53FFF
20 BLOAD "BOOM.RAW",R,P($50000)

MEM 0,3 reserves the first two 8KB blocks of bank 5, 16KB in all, enough for the 12,000-byte sample used below: one and a half seconds at 8 kHz. The BLOAD form follows Dan Sanderson's examples (Dan Sanderson).

Playing it

This routine plays a 12,000-byte, unsigned 8-bit sample at 8 kHz on channel 0. The sample starts at $050000 and its last byte is at $052EDF, of which only the low 16 bits are written. The CPU must be at full speed, its default in MEGA65 mode.

play    lda #$00
        sta $d720       ; stop channel 0 while setting it up
        lda #$80
        sta $d711       ; AUDEN: enable audio DMA

        lda #$00        ; current address: start of sample
        sta $d72a
        sta $d72b
        lda #$05
        sta $d72c
        lda #$00        ; base address: same, in case of looping
        sta $d721
        sta $d722
        lda #$05
        sta $d723

        lda #$df        ; top address: low 16 bits of $052EDF
        sta $d727
        lda #$2e
        sta $d728

        lda #$f2        ; rate: 8 kHz = $000CF2
        sta $d724
        lda #$0c
        sta $d725
        lda #$00
        sta $d726

        lda #$80        ; volume: left side
        sta $d729
        sta $d71c       ; same to the right: centred

        lda #$a2        ; enable, unsigned, 8-bit, no loop
        sta $d720
        rts

The final write starts playback. The routine returns at once, and the sound plays while the program continues. Writing 0 to $D720 stops it early, and calling the routine again restarts the sound, because it resets the current address.

Detecting the end

The hardware sets the stop flag, bit 3 of $D720, when the sample finishes (MEGA65 Book, p. P-20). A game can check it once a frame:

        lda $d720
        and #%00001000  ; CH0STP
        bne finished

Change How
Loop the sound Control value $E2: bit 6 set

With four channels and the rate register acting as a pitch control, this is the basis of a MOD-style music player: short instrument samples, each played at different rates to make different notes. The MEGA65's intro disk includes such a player, Manche (Dan Sanderson).

Summary

  • BASIC 65 plays music with PLAY on SIDs 1 and 3 and effects with SOUND on SIDs 2 and 4, all in the background. SOUND takes raw SID frequency values and durations in jiffies; PLAY strings use notes, modifiers and directives for octave, instrument, volume, filter, vibrato, portamento and looping. TEMPO, ENVELOPE, FILTER, VOL, RPLAY and SOUND CLR complete the set.
  • Four SIDs sit at $D400 and $D420 (the right pair) and $D440 and $D460 (the left pair), mirrored at $D480-$D4FF. SIDMODE at $D63C selects 6581 or 8580 behaviour.
  • Audio DMA, enabled by writing $80 to $D711, plays samples from chip RAM on four channels at $D720-$D75F, using the CPU's idle cycles at full speed. Playback starts at the current address, loops from the base address, and sets a stop flag at the end. Rates are a 24-bit fraction of 40.5 MHz.
  • Stereo comes from channels 0-1 on the left and 2-3 on the right, with $D71C-$D71F feeding each to the opposite side.
  • The mixer combines 16 inputs into 8 outputs through 16-bit coefficients reached with $D6F4 and $D6F5; input 15's coefficient is each output's master volume. The mix belongs to the user, so programs normally adjust their own sources instead.
  • Longer sounds stream through a looping region refilled by DMA from attic RAM; audio DMA keeps playing during ordinary DMA jobs.
